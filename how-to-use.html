How to use the Prompt Builder

The builder turns your UX research into a clear prompt. You paste that prompt into Google Stitch, Lovable or a similar platform, and it makes a clickable prototype for user testing.

1Before you start: do the research first

The builder does not do your UX thinking for you. It asks for things you should already have from earlier work. If you start without research, the AI will fill the gaps with guesses, and you'll end up testing a generic app rather than your design.

Research→Personas→Journey map→User stories→Wireframes→Prompt + prototype→User testing→Iterate

Ready? Tick off what you have

Keep these open while you use the builder.Almost every box can be filled by copying from these documents. If you can't fill a box, that usually means part of your research is missing.

2Using the builder, step by step

Work down the form on the left. The prompt on the right updates as you go. You can type in any box or press its 🎙 microphone button and speak. Press it again to stop.

?Stuck on "What does it do?"

Describe the product in one or two sentences: who it helps, what they can do with it, and why that matters. Leave out the list of features. The screens step is where those go.

[Product] lets [type of user] [do something] so they can [benefit / solve problem].
Too vagueA food app for students.
BetterLets students pre-order food from campus cafés and collect it without queueing between lectures.
Feature listIt has login, a map, chat, payments, reviews, a loyalty scheme and notifications.
BetterHelps first-year students find a quiet study space on campus that's free right now.
Questions to ask yourself
  • What problem did your research show? Which pain point on your journey map does this fix?
  • If you told a friend about it in one breath, what would you say?
  • What does the user have at the end that they didn't have before?

?Stuck on "Target users"

Copy this from your persona. The AI uses it to choose the tone, content, text size and complexity, so be specific about their situation, not just their age.

[Who they are] who [situation / context] and [key frustration or need].
Too broadEveryone. / Young people.
BetterBusy undergraduates aged 18–25 who have short gaps between lectures and hate queueing.
Demographics onlyWomen aged 60+.
BetterRetired people new to smartphones who want to video-call grandchildren but find apps confusing.
No persona yet?

Stop and make one first. It only needs a name, a photo or sketch, a short background, 2–3 goals and 2–3 frustrations, all drawn from your research. You can then type, for example, "Priya, a second-year nursing student who…" into this box.

?Stuck on "Key user story"

Choose the one user story that matters most. It should be the main thing your prototype has to prove works. You'll test this story with real users.

As a [type of user], I want to [goal / action] so that [benefit].
Not a storyThe app should have a basket.
BetterAs a student between lectures, I want to order lunch ahead so that I can collect it without queueing.
Too bigAs a user, I want to manage my whole life.
BetterAs a new student, I want to see which study rooms are free now so that I don't waste time walking around.
How to pick the "key" one
  • Look at your journey map. Which step has the biggest pain point? Pick the story that fixes it.
  • It should be something a tester can finish in 2–5 minutes.
  • If you have several stories, put the others into later prototypes. One focused flow tests better than five half-built ones.

?Stuck on "Screens"

List the screens a user moves through to complete your key user story, in order. Use your wireframes. Each step on your journey map often becomes a screen.

  • Name each screen simply, e.g. Home, Search results, Item detail, Confirmation.
  • In the second box, list the main content and the main button on that screen.
  • About 3–6 screens is right. More than that and the AI is more likely to produce something muddled.
  • Delete the example screens if they don't fit your idea.

?Stuck on "Test task"

Turn your key user story into a realistic task that you'll read out to testers. Give them a goal, but don't tell them which buttons to press.

Gives the answerClick "Menu", then tap the first item, then press "Add".
BetterYou have 15 minutes before your next lecture. Order a vegetarian lunch under £5 from the library café.

In Interactions that must work, list every click needed to finish that task. The AI will then build those parts properly instead of leaving dead buttons.

?Stuck on "Look & feel"

Use your mood board or brand guidelines. Give the mood in 2–3 words (e.g. calm, friendly, minimal), name your main colours, and choose a design system if you have one. The Design systems section below explains how to choose.

3Design systems

A design system is a ready-made set of interface components (buttons, inputs, menus, dialogs, tabs, cards and so on) with guidelines for how and when to use each one. Material Design, shadcn/ui, Carbon, Fluent and Apple's Human Interface Guidelines are all examples.

Why you need to learn how they work

An AI platform will happily place components for you, but it can't judge whether it chose the right one. That's your job as a UX designer. When you know a design system well, you can:

  • Choose the right component for the job. For example, the guidelines say when to use a dialog and when to use a toast message, or when to use tabs and when to use a menu.
  • Spot when the AI gets it wrong, such as a destructive "Delete" button styled as the main action, or checkboxes used where only one choice is allowed.
  • Design every state, not just the default: hover, focus, pressed, disabled, loading, error and empty.
  • Build in accessibility. Good design systems come with tested contrast, focus styles, keyboard behaviour and labelling rules.
  • Use familiar patterns, so your test users already know how things work and your testing measures your design rather than confusion.
  • Write better prompts and follow-ups using the right names, e.g. "Change the filter chips to a segmented control" instead of "make those buttons different".
Before you prototype, spend time in your chosen system's documentation.Find its component pages and read the "usage" or "do and don't" guidance for the components your screens need, such as buttons, forms, navigation, dialogs and feedback messages.

What this means for the builder's design system menu

Each platform handles your choice differently. The hint under the menu in the builder changes to tell you which applies.

PlatformWhat happens when you choose a design system
LovableChoosing a React-based library (shadcn/ui, Material via MUI, Ant Design, Fluent, Carbon, Bootstrap) can change the actual components it builds with. shadcn/ui is Lovable's default.
Google StitchEvery option is a style hint. Stitch draws its own components to match the look. Material Design 3 fits Stitch best because that's what it's built on. You can fine-tune colours, fonts and corner rounding in Stitch's theme settings.
BothApple HIG is a style reference only. It's guidelines rather than code, so the AI copies the look, not the components.

Not sure which to choose? Choose "Let the platform choose" and describe the mood and colours instead. Even then, check the result against a design system's guidelines.

4Using your prompt

One prompt or screen by screen?

  • One prompt puts everything in a single message. Start with this in Lovable.
  • Screen by screen gives you a set of shorter prompts to paste one at a time. This often works better in Stitch, or whenever a big prompt gives a muddled result.

Then

  1. Read the prompt. Does it describe your design? Change any box on the left if it doesn't.
  2. Press Copy and paste the prompt into the platform.
  3. Stitch: choose mobile or web first. You can also upload a photo of your paper wireframe.
  4. Lovable: paste the prompt into the chat box on the home page and send it.
Free plans have limits.Each message uses up credits. Spend time getting the prompt right in the builder before you send it, because that costs nothing.

5After it generates

  1. Try the test task yourself. Can you finish it without getting stuck?
  2. Fix one thing per message, e.g. "Make the 'Pay' button go to the confirmation screen." Several changes in one message often break other things.
  3. Check accessibility: contrast, text size and clear labels.
  4. Check the components against the design system's guidelines. Is each one the right component, used the right way, with all its states?
  5. Get a shareable link (Lovable: Publish; Stitch: preview or export) to use in testing.
  6. Test with real users, write down what happens, and go back to your design. The AI can build a prototype, but only testing tells you whether it's useful, usable and valuable.

6Common problems

The result looks generic and nothing like my wireframes

Add more detail to the Screens descriptions, upload your wireframe image (Stitch), or switch to Screen by screen. Check that your What does it do? and Target users answers are specific.

Buttons don't do anything

List the missing interactions in Interactions that must work, or send a follow-up message such as "When I tap 'Add to basket', update the basket count and show a confirmation."

It added login screens, accounts or a database I didn't ask for

Make sure No login, payments or real backend is ticked in step 6, then tell it: "Remove the login screen. This is a prototype for user testing only."

The microphone button doesn't work

Use Chrome, Edge or Safari, allow microphone access when the browser asks, and open the builder from its web address rather than as a file on your computer. You can also use your computer's own dictation (Windows + H, or press Fn twice on a Mac).

I've run out of credits

Copy your prompt and try another similar platform (e.g. Bolt.new or v0). Choose Other in step 1 and type its name.
